# Maud, the chaplain

**Rule 1: stands.** Maud may name a feeling once, as a scene's turn, and the beat sheet names it in advance (below). She names everyone else's symptoms freely; a symptom is not a feeling, and she knows the difference better than anyone.

**Who.** Kestrow; twenty, the youngest rite-keeper Kestrow has ever sent, because the senior, Osric, was killed on a Kestrow bridge on his way to the hill and nobody else would go. A lazar-house child (STORY draft 7): her order ran the house at Sorrel Bend on the seam, where the garrison's broken men went, and she was its sisters' orphan ward until fourteen, so she learned the basin before the rite, and the rite from the house's old keeper, who said the responses in the old order because that was how he had heard them in the undercroft forty years before. When the armistice dismissed the garrison the order called its sisters home, left the sick with the keeper, and took her, because a ward is property. Devout and stubborn; she says the prayer over the enemy dead because nobody else will. Wants the rite said right, and the sick fed. Won't say that she mends people who haven't asked because it is the one thing in her life nobody can overrule her on (her flaw: duty that is control). Won't say Osric's name to Dunstan, who held the bridge, or Sorrel Bend's to anyone before the Lazar House.

**Image source** (rule 5). **The sickbed:** the basin, the dressing and when to change it, a fever's hours, who has eaten, who hasn't slept, the smell of a wound going bad. **The rite:** the words in order, the response, a word said wrong and said again, the prayer for the dead with the name in it. From Kestrow she takes the cold only as it kills: the old in winter, a chapel too high to heat, frostbite. Never stone, footing or walls (Dunstan's), never the sky or weather (Rook's): the three Kestrow voices split stone, sky and sickbed. Never the kitchen (Brannock's; she counts who ate, he cooks it).

**Shape.** Imperatives in the second person, aimed at the listener's body. **How she corrects people:** the observation, then a task, no softener, and she doesn't wait to see if the task is accepted ("You're favouring the left. Sit."). She asks questions she is about to overrule ("Did you eat? Eat."). Asked about herself, she answers with a question about the asker's body (rule 4). Prayers are said whole, in their own register, and never trimmed for pace.

**Wit:** rare, and only gallows: with the sick and the dying, about dying, never about the rite or the goddess. Nobody in the healthy company hears her joke.

**Names** (rule 8). She spends names on the dead: the prayer has the dead person's name in it, and she asks an enemy's comrades for it before she says it. With the living she uses names only to make them sit still. The captain is "captain" until the raid; after "You stood in front again", Alder once.

**The one named feeling:** proposed for the Lazar House (quest 1), among the sick her order abandoned, fed by the stolen pay. Anger, at her own church, said flat as a diagnosis. The beat sheet confirms it.

**Never.**
- "bless you", "the goddess wills", "have faith", "poor thing"
- "it's not your fault", "you did your best" (she doesn't absolve; she treats)
- a prayer as a closing line of a scene, except over the dead
- **Most at risk:** the gentle healer. She is not gentle; she is right about bodies and bossy about it, and the warmth is in what she does, not how she says it.
- **Second risk:** the observation-then-task as every line. It is her correction shape, not her only shape; between corrections she is twenty and tired.

**Samples** (illustrative; they don't ship and carry no ids).
1. `scene`, after the raid, to the captain (pinned): "You stood in front again. You know they can stand too."
2. `bark`, heals someone who didn't ask, to them: "Hold still. You can be cross after."
3. `bark`, over a fallen enemy, to nobody: "Tell me his name. I'm not saying it without one."
4. `support`, to Dunstan, who has refused the fire: "That's two nights you've slept on the wall. Your hands are going white. Sit by me."
5. `support`, to Keziah, who has given her the blanket: "You're blue at the lips. Take it back, or I'll put it on you while you sleep."
6. `support`, to Pell, who quotes the rite back to her: "That's the old order of the responses. They changed it. They changed it wrong."
7. `scene`, the Lazar House, the abandoned ward, to the captain: "Forty beds. Twelve with someone in them. The pay came this far and stopped at the door."
8. `card`, her epilogue, the Field Surgeon: "Maud left the order and kept the rite. She walks the seam each winter with a basin, and nobody gets to refuse twice."

Sample 2 is the flaw, said as a joke at the patient; sample 6 is her one stubbornness that is about the rite and not a body.

**Average test.** Stock: "Let the goddess's light heal your wounds, my friend." Ours: "That's gone bad. I'll cut it now or the surgeon cuts the arm on Thursday."
